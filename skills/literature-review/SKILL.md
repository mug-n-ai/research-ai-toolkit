# Literature review

## Start with the question

Identify the question the literature needs to answer and what the user wants to produce. For an existing draft, start with the claims that matter to the argument and have weak or incomplete support. For a new review, define the topic and relevant scope, such as the studies, methods, dates or populations it covers.

| Type of review | What to produce |
| --- | --- |
| Exploratory search | A short list of relevant sources, why they matter and what you have read so far. Mark unresolved questions and papers that still need reading. |
| Synthesis or update to a draft | A synthesis organised around the question, with important claims traced to the original evidence. Integrate findings into the existing output when the task calls for edits. |
| Systematic review | Follow the agreed protocol. If it is missing, draft it before formal screening. Record eligibility criteria, databases, complete search strategies, dates, deduplication, screening decisions and exclusion reasons, extraction, and the appraisal and synthesis methods. Report departures from the protocol and any stages still outstanding. Use the reporting guidance appropriate to the field. [PRISMA 2020](https://www.prisma-statement.org/prisma-2020) is one example. |

## Find and read the sources

1. Use the project's existing bibliography or curated collection when available. Reuse earlier readings when the recorded version and coverage support the current question. When searching beyond the collection, record where you searched, the query and filters, the date and any limits on coverage. Use reviews and citation trails to find foundational, recent and competing work. Cite a review directly when the claim concerns its own synthesis.
2. Verify the source identity against a publisher, DOI record or reliable scholarly index. Record the version you read and check for corrections, retractions and relevant supplementary material. Link versions of the same study so the synthesis does not count them twice.
3. Read every source you include as evidence in the review in full, from the introduction and methods through the results, discussion and conclusions. Read the appendices that form part of the paper and any supplementary material that affects the evidence you use. Use abstracts, search snippets and selected passages to find and screen candidates. If you read only part of a source, record which parts you read and leave the full-text review open.
4. Open the original paper and inspect every figure directly, including each panel and its caption. Use the publisher PDF or HTML with the figures, or an authorised author manuscript if that is the version you can access. Check axes, units, legends and the conditions of the comparison. Mark figures you could only read about in a caption or an extracted text as uninspected. Record which figures you inspected and which are missing or unreadable.
5. If you cannot obtain the full text or see its figures, record the gap and leave the source review incomplete. Look for an authorised copy that contains the missing material, ask for a copy the user can share, or use another fully reviewed source that supports the same claim. Leave claims that depend on missing evidence open.

## Check what each source supports

Trace each substantive claim to a section, page, equation, table, figure panel or equivalent locator. Keep the result together with the sample or regime, the assumptions, and the uncertainty that changes how it should be read. Distinguish a direct measurement from an estimate, a model result or the authors' interpretation.

When two studies appear to disagree, compare what they measured or modelled, how they selected the sample, and how they defined the quantity. Check the original evidence on both sides before describing a disagreement. Record what remains unresolved.

Keep a bibliographic record, the extent of reading and the claim check independently traceable. A DOI identifies a source. The evidence entry tells you where to look and how far the result applies. Reuse the project's existing records or use the [optional review record](review-record.md).

## Decide what changes

When several papers address the same question, read them together. For each paper or group, name the passage, conclusion, figure or decision it could change. Record whether to integrate the evidence, cite it briefly, keep it for context, leave it out or revisit it after an access gap. Give the reason in a line.

If the evidence changes the output the user asked you to update, make the edit alongside its citation and evidence record. Use the project's changelog if it tracks substantive changes. Put the finding, citation and scientific limits in the reader-facing text. Keep reading status, editorial decisions and follow-up work in the review record.

Consider a figure when the reader needs to see a comparison the prose alone cannot show clearly. Inspect the relevant panels, axes, units, caption and methods. Decide whether to link to the source figure, reproduce or adapt it with an established basis for reuse, or draw a new plot from checked data or equations. Record the source, the figure's role and the attribution or permissions where relevant.

## Hand over the result

Recheck the claims, numbers and captions against the passages you cite. Say what the search covered, what you could verify, what changed, and which gaps still affect the answer. If you are working through a fixed reading list, report progress by source identity and distinguish what is still unread from what is blocked by access. In a chat without repository access, return the synthesis and the relevant entries of the review record in the conversation.
